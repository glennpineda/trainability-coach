# Load

_Status: reviewed by Glenn, 17 Oct 2026 (from the first book)._

Load sets how much tension the working muscle has to make, and that tension
drives both strength and size. It also sets how hard the nervous system has
to work.

## Heavy loads train the nervous system

Loads of about 85–100% (1–5 reps) mainly improve how the nervous system runs
the muscle: a stronger signal, more of the muscle switched on together,
faster firing, and less of the muscle's own protective braking.

## Ways to progress

- **Straight-line progression**: a little heavier each session or week. Simple
  and safe early on, and right for the first stages of rehabilitation. It runs
  out: as the load climbs, volume has to fall, size gains stall, and the risk
  of overdoing it rises.
- **Waves within a session**: for experienced people only. Alternate heavier
  and lighter sets; after a heavier set, the lighter one feels lighter and
  goes better than it did before.
- **Waves across weeks**: alternate weeks or blocks of more volume (lighter)
  with more intensity (heavier) so size and strength are both kept (see
  [periodization.md](periodization.md)).

## Loading methods

Pyramids (load up, down or both), single and multiple sets, supersets, drop
sets, circuits and push-pull pairings are all valid ways to overload. Choose
by goal and by what the person tolerates; rotate them for variety.

## Slow lowering (eccentric) work

Muscles are strongest while lowering, so training the lowering on purpose,
sometimes with more than the person can lift, is its own method.

**What it is good for**

- Slowing down limbs and implements, and landing from jumps.
- Preventing and rehabilitating overuse injuries, which often come from a
  muscle that is weak at braking. A standard tool in rehab.
- Getting past a plateau, and building muscle quickly.
- Strength gained lowering carries over to lifting better than the other way
  round.

**Care with it**

- Heavy lowering work (more than the person can lift) is for people with
  **1–2 years of solid training**, rehab aside. Before that, simply lowering
  every rep under control is enough.
- Heavy lowering needs skilled spotters, and the heavier it is the more they
  matter.
- It makes people sore for days. Plan recovery, and keep it **well away from
  competitions** or tests (a week or more).
- Use **1–6 reps** when the load is above what the person can lift; start
  around 4–6.
- Run it in **blocks of about six weeks**, then a break of about two.
- It can add muscle, which is not what somebody who needs to stay light
  wants.
- Keep it a small part of teenagers' training.

**How to build it up**, in three stages; the trainer sets the numbers
(Glenn, 17 Oct 2026: "Principle only"):

1. Lower every rep under control. Enough for anybody in their first year or
   two.
2. A few extra lowering reps after the lifting reps are done, with help to
   lift.
3. Lowering loads heavier than the person can lift, for the experienced only.
