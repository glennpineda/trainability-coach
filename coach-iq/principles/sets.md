# Sets

_Status: reviewed, 17 Oct 2026. From: Program Design (Paul Chek, 2011)._

## Reps and sets go opposite ways

The fewer the reps, the more sets: heavy work of 1–5 reps can take 5 or more
sets; sets of 15 and up rarely need more than 2–4.

## Building up

- **The first few weeks**: 1–2 sets per exercise are enough. Early gains come
  from the nervous system learning the movement, not from more work.
- **Over about 6–8 weeks**, build to around 3 sets.
- **Once a base is built**, 3–6 sets with set rests beat a single set for
  most goals. Increase step by step.
- **As a person becomes more trained**, they need more sets to keep improving.

## Which muscles

- Muscles that rarely work hard (the neck, the inner thigh) respond to few
  sets: 1–3.
- Smaller muscles (the triceps) recover faster than big ones (the thighs);
  big ones need more total work to improve.

## Per session

- Keep a session's total under about **20–25 working sets**; well beyond
  30 the quality of the work falls.
- The more time somebody spends on their sport or other training, the less
  room there is for sets in the gym.
- **Most sessions take about 45–75 minutes**: a rough guide for planning, not
  a limit. The set count is what matters.

## A set window

Write sets as a range, such as **1–4**: the low number is the safe minimum
on a day the person is tired or sore; the high number is the full dose for
this phase. It gives room to adjust without rewriting the program.

## When somebody hasn't recovered

**Cut sets, not intensity**, when the goal is strength: keep the loads, do
less of them. Sets are the main dial for managing fatigue.

## Short, very hard sessions

Brief, one-all-out-set training works for a while mostly because it is a
break from higher volume. Use it as one phase among others, not as the whole
plan: a trained person needs repeated sets to keep adapting.
