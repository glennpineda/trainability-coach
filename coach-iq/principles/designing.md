# Designing a program

_Status: reviewed, 17 Oct 2026. From: Program Design (Paul Chek, 2011)._

## Before the first session

Ask about the client's health first, with a health questionnaire: past and
current injuries, illness, medication, how they sleep, eat and live. It shows
what is likely to get in the way of the program, and when somebody needs a
doctor's go-ahead before starting (see the conditions table).

## Guidelines

1. **Set goals for the long term and for each session**, realistic and
   attainable, and work to meet them.
2. **Record everything**: load, tempo, rest, reps and sets done, and any aches
   or pains that got in the way. A program can only be judged, or repeated,
   from the record.
3. **Watch how performance and fatigue move** from session to session and
   between programs; they say whether the load is right.
4. **Progress on purpose.** Add work only as fast as the person recovers and
   adapts to it.
5. **Build on a general foundation** before anything specialised (see
   [starting-out.md](starting-out.md)).
6. **Plan the whole year.** Avoid long breaks: what is gained is lost faster
   than it was built. Plan lighter periods instead of stopping.
7. **Warm up and cool down** every session.
8. **Test regularly**, or compete, to know whether the program works.
   Beginners gain a lot from testing themselves often.
9. **Come back carefully** after injury or illness, with a program for coming
   back, not the old one.

## Energy: what the goal or sport needs

Match reps, sets and rest to how the client's activity supplies energy:

- **Short, all-out bursts** (up to about 10 seconds: a sprint, a jump, a
  lift, a throw) run on the body's immediate stores.
- **Hard efforts of roughly 10 seconds to 2 minutes** (a 400 m run, a long
  rally, a hard set of many reps) rely on breaking down sugar without enough
  oxygen, with the burning feeling and fatigue that come with it.
- **Longer, steadier work** (distance running, rowing, cycling, swimming,
  most daily life) runs on oxygen.

Most activities mix all three. A sprinter's program leans on short sets with
long rests; a distance athlete's on longer sets, shorter rests and more
endurance work. For a general client, the goal (strength, muscle, fat loss,
endurance) plays the part of the sport.

## Choosing the variables

Each variable has its own file: [reps](reps.md), [sets](sets.md),
[load](load.md), [tempo](tempo.md), [rest](rest.md). They depend on each
other: more reps means fewer sets and lighter load; heavier load means fewer
reps and longer rest. Choose reps first; the others follow.
