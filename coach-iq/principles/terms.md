# Terms

_Status: reviewed, 17 Oct 2026. From: Program Design (Paul Chek, 2011)._

## The variables of a program

A program is made of a handful of choices for each exercise, changed on
purpose to get a result:

- **Reps**: how many times in a row. The choice that shapes the result most.
- **Sets**: how many times that group of reps is done.
- **Load**: the weight, or the resistance a band, machine or the body gives.
- **Tempo**: how fast each part of a rep is done, and any pause.
- **Rest**: the time between sets.

Around them sit **frequency** (sessions a week), **exercise choice** and
**exercise order**. Change one of them and the program has changed: a
program's results only mean something if these are written down and kept.

## Volume, load, intensity and density

- **Volume** is how much work is done: total reps, or total weight lifted
  (reps × load), in a set, session or week. Look at reps and load together;
  either alone misleads.
- **Intensity** is how hard the work is against the person's current best:
  the load as a share of their one-rep maximum (1RM), or how close to failure
  a set goes. It is the variable that matters most for strength.
- **Density** is how much work is packed into the time: short rests, circuits,
  "high-intensity" classes. What fitness marketing calls high intensity is
  usually high density.

### Effort: reps in reserve

How hard a set is can be written as how many more reps the person could have
done with good form when they stopped: **reps in reserve**. "−1" or "1 in
reserve" is one rep short of failure; "−2" is two. It works without knowing
anybody's 1RM, and is how Coach IQ talks about effort.

## Training age and level

**Training age** is how long somebody has trained consistently with
progressing loads, not how old they are: a 30-year-old who has lifted
steadily for two years has a training age of two.

| | Training age |
|---|---|
| Beginner | under 1 year |
| Intermediate | 1–2 years |
| Advanced | over 2 years |

**One scale for both** (Glenn, 17 Oct 2026: "One shared scale"). A client's
level and an exercise's level (the skill and strength it needs) use the same
three words: a beginner client starts from beginner exercises, and moves up
as their training age and skill grow. The trainer's judgement of a particular
client still wins over the calendar.

## Kinds of strength

- **Maximal strength**: the most force somebody can produce in one all-out
  effort, whatever the time it takes.
  - **Absolute**: that force regardless of body weight (matters where body
    mass helps: throwing, pushing, many contact positions).
  - **Relative**: that force for the person's body weight (matters where the
    body itself is moved: climbing, gymnastics, running, weight-class sports,
    everyday life).
- **Optimal strength**: as much as the activity needs; past it, more strength
  no longer helps performance, and time is better spent elsewhere.
- **Strength endurance**: keeping up muscular effort over time. Heavier,
  shorter efforts (a wrestler) and lighter, longer ones (a distance runner,
  rower or swimmer) are both kinds of it.
- **Speed-strength**: producing force quickly.
  - **Starting**: force right at the start of a movement.
  - **Explosive**: continuing to build force quickly once moving.
  - **Reactive**: switching fast from lowering to lifting, as in a jump.

## How muscles act

- **Concentric**: the muscle shortens while working (lifting).
- **Eccentric**: the muscle lengthens while working, braking the movement
  (lowering). A muscle is strongest this way.
- **Isometric**: the muscle works without the joint moving (holding).
- Real movement is mostly a mix of speeds and tensions ("dynamic"); a
  truly constant-tension or constant-speed contraction is rare outside
  machines.

## How muscles work together

- **Prime movers** do the movement; **opposing muscles** sit across the joint
  from them; **stabilizers** hold a body part steady so others can move it
  (the muscles around the shoulder blade during an arm raise);
  **neutralizers** cancel a movement that isn't wanted.
- **Coordination within a muscle** is how much of it can be switched on at
  once. A beginner uses noticeably less of a muscle than a trained person
  can, which is why early gains are fast and mostly in the nervous system.
- **Coordination between muscles** is how well they take turns and share the
  work. With practice, opposing muscles get out of the way, and movement gets
  faster and stronger.

## Station and circuit training

- **Station**: one exercise, rest, repeat for its sets, then the next.
- **Circuit**: several exercises one after another with little rest, a longer
  rest after the round.
